Refractory Noninfectious Uveitis: RUBI Trial of Adalimumab, Tocilizumab, and Anakinra

📑 Multicentre Bayesian randomised trial · Saadoun et al. 2026 · Am J Ophthalmol · 27 French centres · 112 participants

Refractory noninfectious uveitis requires a balance between controlling inflammation and reducing corticosteroid exposure. The 2026 RUBI trial directly compared three biologics: adalimumab and tocilizumab showed no clear difference on the primary endpoint, while anakinra was stopped early for insufficient efficacy. It is an informative exploratory study, not a ready-made drug-selection formula for every patient.

⚠️ Before we begin: This article explains a single study for general education and is not individual medical advice. Uveitis has many causes, and infectious and noninfectious disease require different approaches. Biologic therapy should be evaluated by an ophthalmologist experienced in uveitis together with relevant specialists. Do not stop, add, or switch medication on your own.

Study highlights

30-second summary

  • Participants were adults with active refractory intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis, not isolated anterior uveitis.
  • Primary endpoint response: adalimumab 16% (7/44), tocilizumab 14% (7/50); the trial showed no clear superiority.
  • Tocilizumab showed secondary signals worth studying for steroid reduction and macular oedema, but superiority has not been proven.
  • The Taiwan NHI document explicitly lists criteria for adalimumab in paediatric noninfectious uveitis aged 2-17 years; this cannot be directly extrapolated to the adult trial population.

1. Why study biologic therapy?

Uveitis is not one disease but a group of inflammatory eye disorders. This article discusses noninfectious uveitis (NIU), in which inflammation is not directly caused by bacteria, viruses, or other infections. It may be classified as anterior, intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis by location.

Corticosteroids often suppress inflammation quickly, but prolonged exposure also carries risks. When disease continues to relapse despite corticosteroids and conventional immunosuppressive therapy, clinicians may consider other ways to control inflammation while reducing steroid exposure. Biologic therapy is one such research direction.

2. How was the RUBI trial designed?

The RUBI trial by Saadoun and colleagues was a prospective, multicentre, open-label Bayesian randomised controlled trial conducted across 27 French centres. It included 112 adults followed for 16 weeks. Participants had active refractory noninfectious intermediate, posterior, or panuveitis.

AdalimumabTNF-α80 mg loading dose, then 40 mg every other week44
AnakinraIL-1100 mg daily18

These were subcutaneous doses used in the trial protocol, not instructions for readers to adjust medication themselves. The study also used a standardised steroid-tapering strategy, aiming to reduce prednisone to no more than 0.1 mg/kg/day during weeks 12-16 when disease control allowed.

3. Why was the primary endpoint so strict?

The primary endpoint did not rely on one scan or simply ask whether symptoms improved. At week 16, participants had to meet both conditions: at least a two-step improvement in vitreous haze and prednisone reduced to no more than 0.1 mg/kg/day. The primary endpoint percentage therefore does not mean that only these participants had any clinical improvement.

Week-16 outcome Adalimumab Tocilizumab
Primary endpoint achieved7/44（16%）7/50（14%）
Vitreous haze ≤ 135/44（80%）41/50（82%）
Absence of macular oedema21/44（48%）27/50（54%）
Prednisone ≤ 0.1 mg/kg/day26/44（59%）37/50（74%）

⚠️ Correct interpretation: no drug was proven superior

The difference in the primary endpoint between adalimumab and tocilizumab was -2.0%, with a 95% credible interval of -16.8% to +12.3%. This exploratory trial showed no clear superiority and was not sufficient to prove that both drugs are fully equivalent for every patient, every outcome, or long-term safety.

4. Are there signals worth following for tocilizumab?

Yes, but the interpretation should remain cautious. A higher proportion of participants in the tocilizumab group reduced prednisone to no more than 0.1 mg/kg/day by week 16, and the median change in central retinal thickness was also greater. This makes tocilizumab a reasonable candidate for further study in macular oedema and steroid reduction.

However, these were secondary outcomes. The trial was not specifically designed to prove that tocilizumab is better for macular oedema, and the sample size was not sufficient for a definitive conclusion. The appropriate wording is "a signal worth further study," not "proven to be more effective."

5. Why was anakinra stopped early?

An interim analysis found insufficient efficacy for anakinra in this study setting, so enrolment to that arm was stopped early according to the protocol. This is important for research direction: in adults with refractory noninfectious non-anterior uveitis, anakinra did not show an efficacy signal strong enough to continue in this trial.

This does not mean that anakinra is ineffective for every disease, every uveitis subtype, or every patient. The conclusion should stay within the population actually studied.

6. Safety: what was observed over 16 weeks?

Among 112 participants, 58 (52%) experienced mild-to-moderate adverse events. Injection-related local reactions were most common, followed by infections. Serious adverse events occurred in 9 participants (8%). The study also recorded severe COVID-19, pneumonia, drug-induced hepatitis, and other events.

⚠️ This small short trial cannot rank the safety of the three drugs

Group sizes were small and follow-up lasted only 16 weeks. Event counts support discussion but cannot prove that one drug is safer or more dangerous. Individual assessment still depends on infection risk, comorbidities, previous treatment, and monitoring capacity.

7. What does this study not answer?

LimitationHow to interpret it

Heterogeneous subtypesCannot reliably determine the best drug for a particular uveitis subtype.

8. Taiwan NHI: separate the trial population from reimbursement criteria

In the 23 April 2026 version of Taiwan's National Health Insurance drug reimbursement rules provided for this article, the explicit uveitis provision is for adalimumab in paediatric noninfectious uveitis aged 2-17 years. Prior review is required, together with criteria for active disease and failure of standard therapy. Initial approval is limited to 24 weeks, with reassessment every 24 weeks for continued use.

⚠️ Do not translate an adult trial directly into Taiwan NHI coverage

The RUBI trial studied adults aged 18 years or older with refractory non-anterior uveitis. The Taiwan NHI provision explicitly addresses adalimumab for paediatric noninfectious uveitis aged 2-17 years. These documents answer different questions. The provided reimbursement document also does not establish criteria for adalimumab in adult noninfectious uveitis or for tocilizumab or anakinra in uveitis. Actual applications should follow the latest NHI rules and the treating team's assessment at the time of care.

9. Frequently asked questions

Q: Has tocilizumab been proven better than adalimumab?
A: No. The primary endpoint showed no clear superiority. Tocilizumab showed signals worth investigating in some secondary outcomes, but this is not proof that it is more effective.
Q: Does early stopping mean anakinra never works?
A: That extrapolation is not justified. The trial shows only that anakinra had insufficient efficacy to continue enrolment in this adult refractory noninfectious non-anterior uveitis setting.
Q: Is adalimumab always reimbursed for adult uveitis in Taiwan?
A: This article cannot support that conclusion. The provided 23 April 2026 reimbursement document explicitly lists criteria for paediatric noninfectious uveitis aged 2-17 years. Adult cases should be assessed using the latest NHI rules and the treating team's evaluation.

10. Conclusion: place the evidence in context

The value of the RUBI trial is its direct comparison of three biologics within one adult refractory noninfectious uveitis framework. It suggests that adalimumab and tocilizumab both merit further study; anakinra lacked a sufficient efficacy signal in this setting; and tocilizumab produced signals worth following for macular oedema and steroid reduction.

It does not tell us that every patient should start with one fixed drug, that two drugs are proven fully equivalent, or that an adult trial can be converted directly into Taiwan NHI coverage. The most useful next step for patients remains a structured discussion with their care team about uveitis subtype, previous treatment, imaging changes, and comorbidities.
